Keno guide

The history of Keno

From a Han dynasty fundraising scheme to four draws a day in New Zealand — and why the game still uses 80 numbers.

Ancient China

Keno is usually traced to China around two thousand years ago, in a form that had nothing to do with numbers. The traditional account has a game of chance run during the Han dynasty to raise funds — often linked, though the link is more legend than record, to building work on the Great Wall.

The early game is said to have used characters rather than digits: players marked a selection from the first 120 characters of the Thousand Character Classic, a well-known text in which no character repeats. That property is what made it usable — every character was a unique, unambiguous symbol, exactly as numbers are today.

Treat the specifics with caution. Much of what circulates about Keno's origins comes from twentieth-century gambling literature rather than contemporary sources, and the fundraising-for-the-Great-Wall story in particular is repeated far more often than it is evidenced.

How it reached the West

The game travelled with Chinese migration in the nineteenth century, arriving in the United States with labourers working on the railroads and in the goldfields. It was played in Chinese communities well before it was played anywhere else in America.

It was known for a time as the Chinese lottery, and the characters were gradually replaced with numbers as it moved into a wider market.

Why 80 numbers?

The pool shrank as the game westernised — from 120 characters, to 90, to the 80 that most of the world now uses. There is no deep mathematical reason for 80. It is a practical number: large enough that the odds are long and the prizes can be substantial, small enough that a physical ball machine or a paper card stays manageable.

The 20-drawn-from-80 structure gives the game its defining property: any single number appears 25% of the time. One in four. That figure runs through everything on this site — it is the baseline our statistics converge on and the number that makes "hot" and "cold" streaks meaningless.

Not every market uses 80. France's Keno Gagnant à Vie draws 20 from 70, which changes every odds figure. If you find a Keno odds table online, check which pool it assumes before trusting it.

The name

"Keno" most likely comes from French quine, meaning a set of five winning numbers, or from Latin quini, five each. The game itself is Chinese; the word that stuck to it is European — a fair reflection of a game that changed hands several times on its way to the modern form.

Keno in New Zealand

Keno is operated here by Lotto New Zealand, alongside Lotto, Powerball, Bullseye and Instant Kiwi. It sits apart from the rest of that range in one important way: frequency. Lotto draws twice a week; Keno draws four times a day — 10:01am, 1:01pm, 3:01pm and 6:01pm. See the draw schedule.

New Zealand's version keeps the standard 20-from-80 structure, adds a per-draw multiplier, and lets players choose both their spot count (one to ten) and their stake.

What has not changed

Two thousand years of migration, three different pool sizes and a change of alphabet later, the mechanic is identical: a fixed pool, a random subset drawn from it, and a prize scaled to how much of your selection came up.

So is the mathematics. A Han dynasty player marking characters faced the same structural problem as someone tapping numbers on a phone in Auckland this afternoon — the draw does not know what it did last time, and no method of choosing has ever changed that. Our analysis of 220 draws is only the most recent demonstration of a very old fact.
