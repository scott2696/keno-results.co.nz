Pokies are the most played casino game in New Zealand and the most misunderstood. That is not an accident of education: they are designed to be legible as entertainment and opaque as arithmetic. This page is the arithmetic.

A note on the word first. In New Zealand they are pokies, not slots. If a page aimed at Kiwi players says “slots” throughout, it was written for another market and localised with a find-and-replace, which tells you roughly how much of the rest was checked.

How do online pokies work?

An online pokie is a random number generator with a graphic layer on top. When you press spin, the outcome is determined immediately by a number drawn from a continuously running sequence. The reels then animate a result that has already been decided.

Three consequences follow, and they dispose of most of the folklore:

  • Stopping the reels early changes nothing. The result existed before the animation started. The stop button shortens the animation.
  • The machine is never “due”. Each draw is independent of every previous one. A game that has paid nothing for two hours has exactly the same probability on the next spin as one that just paid a jackpot.
  • Timing, bet pattern and “warming up” do nothing. There is no state carried between spins except, on some games, an explicitly displayed feature meter.

That last exception is the only real one. Where a game shows a meter filling toward a feature, that is genuine carried state and it is disclosed on screen. Everything undisclosed is not state, it is pattern-finding.

Highest RTP online pokies NZ: reading the number

Return to player is the share of total turnover a game pays back across its full theoretical cycle. Its complement is the house edge: a 96% pokie costs about NZ$4 per NZ$100 staked, in the long run and on average. The full explanation, including why the long run is longer than most people assume, is on payouts and RTP.

The pokie-specific warning is this: a number of studios ship the same title in several RTP builds, and the operator picks which to run. The artwork, the paytable and the features are identical; the return is not. Read the figure in the game’s own information panel on the site you are playing on, because a review page’s figure describes a different deployment.

Pokie features, and which ones cost you

The vocabulary is worth knowing because two of these are frequently misunderstood in ways that cost money.

  • Wild — substitutes for other symbols. Expanding, sticky and walking wilds are variations on where and how long it applies.
  • Scatter — pays or triggers regardless of position, usually the route into free spins.
  • Free spins — the feature most games concentrate their return inside. On a high-volatility title, a large share of the theoretical RTP lives here, which is why base play can feel barren.
  • Multiplier — multiplies a win. Progressive multipliers that climb through a feature are the main driver of very large results.
  • Cascade or avalanche — winning symbols are removed and replaced, allowing chained wins from one spin.
  • Gamble feature — double-or-nothing on a win. Usually a coin-flip or card-colour bet, usually at a higher house edge than the base game, and always optional. Declining it is the correct default.
  • Bonus buy — pay a multiple of your stake to enter the feature immediately. Covered below, because it is the one that changes your exposure most.

How to read a pokie paytable

Almost nobody opens it, and it takes thirty seconds. Four things are in there that change what you should do.

  1. The RTP. Usually in a game-information or rules tab. This is the figure that applies to the build you are actually playing, which may not be the one a review page quoted.
  2. The maximum win, expressed as a multiple of stake. A 50,000× cap tells you the game is extremely high volatility before you have spun once.
  3. Whether all paylines must be active. On some games reducing lines reduces your win chances faster than it reduces your stake, which makes it a worse bet rather than a cheaper one.
  4. How the feature is triggered, and whether the trigger frequency is published. Where it is, it is the single best volatility indicator available.

The last row is the one that matters and the one that is never advertised. On a high-volatility game the mean and the median part company: the average is propped up by rare outcomes, so most sessions land below it. Nothing is malfunctioning. You are simply experiencing the ordinary part of a distribution whose exceptional part pays for the headline figure.

Hit frequency, and losses disguised as wins

Hit frequency is the proportion of spins returning anything at all. It is not generosity, and the gap between the two is deliberate.

On a multi-line game you stake all lines at once. A NZ$2 spin across twenty lines that hits one line for NZ$0.60 has “won” — and cost you NZ$1.40, with lights and a sound to mark the occasion. The industry term for this is a loss disguised as a win, it is a well documented design pattern, and it is why hit frequency can be high on a game returning very little. Judge the balance, not the celebration.

Paylines, ways to win and Megaways pokies

How a game counts a win changes the stake, not the edge.

  • Fixed paylines — defined patterns across the reels, all normally active, with the total stake divided among them.
  • Ways to win — any matching symbols on adjacent reels count regardless of position. 243 or 1,024 ways is a counting method, not a generosity measure.
  • Megaways and variable reels — the number of symbols per reel changes each spin, producing a large and varying number of ways. These are usually high-volatility designs with the return concentrated in the feature.
  • Cluster pays — wins formed by adjacent groups rather than lines, typically with cascading replacements.

None of these structures is better value than another. They are different shapes of the same fixed edge — the same thing that is true of Keno spot counts, where changing the spot count changes the shape of the prize ladder and never the edge.

Progressive jackpot pokies

A progressive jackpot is funded by a slice of every stake, pooled across players and often across operators. That has a specific consequence worth understanding.

The advertised RTP of a progressive game includes the jackpot contribution. Since the overwhelming majority of players will never win the pool, the return actually experienced by a typical player is lower than the headline figure — the difference sits waiting in a prize almost nobody collects. A 96% progressive with 2% of turnover going into the pool behaves for practical purposes like a 94% game with a lottery ticket attached.

Two more things worth checking. Some progressives require a maximum bet to qualify for the top prize, so a smaller stake can make the jackpot unreachable while still funding it. And large wins may be subject to a withdrawal cap that pays in instalments — a clause to find on the withdrawals page before you play, not after.

Are online pokies legal in NZ, and how do they differ from pub pokies?

Playing has never been an offence for a New Zealander. The operators are the party the law concerns itself with, and the full picture is on licensed online casinos.

The machines in New Zealand pubs and clubs are a genuinely different regulated product. Class 4 gaming machines under the Gambling Act 2003 are the most tightly controlled gambling in the country: stakes and prizes are capped, returns are set by gazetted game rules rather than chosen by the venue, societies must return a minimum share of proceeds to community purposes, and a venue must exclude a player who asks to be excluded.

Online pokies are supplied by international studios to operators licensed abroad. The games themselves are audited to a high standard by independent test houses; what differs is the counterparty holding your money, the absence of a capped stake, and the fact that nobody is standing next to you.

Is there any online pokies strategy that works?

Not for winning, and the distinction between that and “nothing helps” is where the useful part sits.

Nothing changes the odds of a spin. Not stake size, not timing, not the order you press things, not stopping the reels, not switching games after a loss, not any of the betting systems sold for this purpose. The outcome is drawn independently every time, and there is no sequence to exploit because there is no sequence.

What you can control is how much turnover you generate and therefore how much the edge collects, and those are large effects. A strategy that halves your spins per hour halves your expected cost exactly. That is not a way to win; it is a way to buy the same number of hours of entertainment for half the money, which is a real and achievable thing.

The systems that do not work, and why

Martingale — doubling after a loss — fails on pokies for two independent reasons: the maximum bet caps the sequence, and the wins are not even-money, so the arithmetic the system depends on does not apply in the first place. Every variant of “increase after a loss” shares the same flaw: it increases turnover, and turnover is precisely what the house edge is charged on.
