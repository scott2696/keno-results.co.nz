Which online casino pays out the most?

It is the most asked question about online casinos NZ-wide, and the honest answer is that no casino pays out the most — games do. Whatever a site looks like, the money moves according to the house edge of the games you play. These are not estimates — for the table games they fall out of the rules arithmetically, the same way Keno’s odds do.

Two readings of that table are worth more than any casino review.

First, the spread between the top and bottom rows is nearly thirty-fold. Choosing single-zero roulette over double-zero halves your expected cost for an otherwise identical game, and it is a choice you make at the table with no skill required. The tie bet at baccarat costs roughly fourteen times what the banker bet costs at the same table, in the same game, on the same hand.

Second, none of these numbers is a prediction about tonight. A 2.70% edge says that across a very large number of spins the wheel keeps about $2.70 of every $100 staked. Across fifty spins it says almost nothing, which is exactly why the edge is survivable as entertainment and fatal as a plan.

Online gambling NZ: how it differs from what is already legal here

New Zealanders have had legal gambling for decades, and it is worth being clear about where online casino games sit relative to it, because the differences are not the ones people expect.

The pokies in pubs and clubs are Class 4 gaming machines under the Gambling Act 2003, and they are the most tightly controlled form of gambling in the country — capped stakes, capped prizes, mandatory returns to community purposes, and venues that must exclude a player on request. Their return percentages are set by gazetted game rules rather than chosen by the venue.

The six land-based casinos, SkyCity among them, hold casino venue licences and operate under conditions the Gambling Commission has a role in. They are physical, staffed, and subject to on-site inspection.

Lotto NZ’s games — Lotto, Powerball, Keno, Bullseye, Instant Kiwi — are run by a Crown entity whose surplus goes to the Lottery Grants Board rather than to shareholders. That is why a Keno ticket and a pokie spin are not the same transaction even where the arithmetic is similar.

Online casino gambling has, until now, sat outside all of that: offered from offshore, unreachable by the regulator, and with no New Zealand complaints path. The 2026 Act is an attempt to pull it inside the tent. Whether it succeeds is a fair question, but the direction is clear, and the practical effect from December is that the set of sites lawfully able to take your money gets much smaller.

One thing does not change: prize money is not taxed in New Zealand. Lotto NZ states prizes are paid in full, and gambling winnings are generally not income for tax purposes for a recreational player. What the money earns afterwards — interest, dividends, rent — is taxable in the ordinary way. That is general information rather than tax advice.

Playing safely

I run a site about a gambling product, and I think that obliges me to be direct rather than bury this in a footer. Every figure above points the same way: these games are built so that, across enough play, they keep more than they return. That is the design, not a scandal. It becomes a problem when someone plays as though it were not true.

The clearest warning sign is chasing — playing to win back a loss rather than because you wanted to play, because it means the loss is now making the decisions. The others worth taking seriously are hiding how much you play, using money meant for something else, and believing a number or a machine is due.
