Gaming in New Zealand

Who regulates gambling in New Zealand

The Gambling Act 2003, the four classes of gambling, the Department of Internal Affairs, and why sports betting sits under a different law entirely.

{subnav}

When I started publishing Keno results I assumed the regulatory picture would be simple: one law, one regulator. It is not, and the structure explains a lot about why some things are freely available here and others are not.

The founding principle

The Gambling Act 2003 works by prohibition with exceptions. Gambling is unlawful in New Zealand unless the Act specifically authorises it. There is no general licence to run a gambling business and then be supervised; there is a defined list of what is permitted, and anything outside it is illegal regardless of how it is dressed up.

That is why New Zealand has no domestic online casino industry to speak of, and why the 2026 Act was needed to create one rather than simply to tidy up an existing market.

The four classes

The Act sorts gambling by how much money is involved and who keeps it.

Classes of gambling under the Gambling Act 2003
ClassWhat it covers
Class 1Small-scale, no licence needed. A raffle at a school fair, where prizes are modest and nobody profits.
Class 2Larger community fundraising. Bigger prize pools, still run by societies rather than commercially.
Class 3Substantial prizes, licence required. Large charitable lotteries sit here.
Class 4Gaming machines outside casinos — the pokies in pubs and clubs. The most tightly controlled, and the source of most harm-related regulation.

Casinos are handled separately again. New Zealand has six licensed land-based casinos, and the Act prevents new casino licences being issued — the number is capped rather than open to competition.

Who does what

  • The Department of Internal Affairs is the regulator. It licenses, inspects, enforces, and now administers the online casino regime. If something is going wrong with a gambling operator, this is who hears about it.
  • The Gambling Commission is the independent body that hears appeals against the Department’s decisions and handles casino licensing matters. It is a check on the regulator rather than a second regulator.
  • Lotto New Zealand is a Crown entity, not a private company. It runs Lotto, Powerball, Strike, Keno, Bullseye and Instant Kiwi, and its profits go to the Lottery Grants Board for distribution to community causes.
  • TAB NZ holds the monopoly on sports and racing betting.

Why sports betting is different

Betting on sport and racing is governed by the Racing Industry Act 2020 alongside the Gambling Act, not by the casino provisions. That separation matters more than it sounds. It means a change to online casino law — like the one arriving on 1 December 2026 — leaves sports betting untouched, and it means the two are regulated with different objectives.

What this means for an online casino player

The short answer is that the Department of Internal Affairs is who you complain to about a licensed operator, and that until a site holds a licence here there is no New Zealand body in that role at all — which is the single most useful thing to understand before depositing anywhere. Where the money goes

This is the part I found genuinely surprising. Lotto NZ is state-owned, and its surplus does not go to shareholders. In its 2025 financial year it returned a near-record $395 million to New Zealand communities through the Lottery Grants Board — funding sport, arts, heritage and community organisations.

Class 4 gaming machines work on a similar principle: societies operating pokies must return a minimum share of proceeds to authorised community purposes. It is also where most of the documented harm sits, which is the tension at the centre of New Zealand gambling policy and not one I can resolve in a paragraph.
