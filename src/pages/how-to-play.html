Keno guide

How to play Keno

Keno is one of the simplest lottery games to understand: pick some numbers, wait for the draw, count your matches. Here is the whole game, including the parts most guides skip.

{subnav}

The game in one paragraph

Every draw, 20 numbers are selected at random from a pool of 1 to 80. Before the draw you choose between one and ten numbers of your own — these are your spots. Your prize depends on how many of your spots appear among the 20 drawn, how much you staked, and the multiplier attached to that draw.

That is genuinely all of it. Everything below is detail on the choices you make within that frame.

Step by step

  1. Choose how many spots to play. Anywhere from one to ten. This is the most consequential decision on the ticket, because it determines the entire prize ladder — see choosing a spot count below.
  2. Choose your numbers. Mark them yourself, or take a random selection — often called a Dip. Neither method affects your odds. Our number generator will produce a line and show you its true odds beside it.
  3. Set your stake. Wagers run from NZ$1 to NZ$100 per ticket, and the prize scales with what you put on. A $2 ticket pays twice what the same result pays on a $1 ticket.
  4. Choose how many draws to enter. One draw, or the same numbers across several consecutive draws.
  5. Keep your ticket. It is your proof of entry, and you need it to claim.

Choosing a spot count

This is where most people go wrong, because the intuition is backwards. Playing more spots does not improve your chances. It makes the top prize dramatically harder to reach while widening the ladder of smaller prizes beneath it.

A ten-spot ticket asks for a one-in-nine-million event. A two-spot asks for one in seventeen. What the ten-spot buys you is more ways to win something small — on most paytables it pays on partial matches well below the top tier, sometimes even for matching nothing at all.

Neither is "better". They are different shapes of the same fixed house edge. Pick the one whose ladder you actually want.

How prizes are structured

Four things set the size of a win: your spot count, how many you matched, your stake, and the draw's multiplier. That is why no single prize table can answer "how much did I win" — the same three matches are worth different amounts on different tickets.

New Zealand Keno caps the payout at NZ$1 million per ticket. Full detail on how Keno prizes work.

The multiplier

Every draw carries a multiplier that scales what a winning ticket pays. Across the 220-plus draws in our archive it has ranged from ×1.5 to ×10, averaging about ×2. It applies to the whole draw — everyone playing that draw gets the same one.

The thing to understand: the multiplier changes what a win pays, never how likely a win is. A ×10 draw and a ×1.5 draw have identical odds. See the multiplier page for the observed distribution.

When draws happen

Four times a day, every day, at 10:01am, 1:01pm, 3:01pm and 6:01pm New Zealand time. We have recorded exactly 55 draws at each of those times across a 56-day span, weekends and holidays included. See the draw schedule.

That frequency matters more than it looks. Four draws a day is roughly 28 a week and 1,460 a year, and the odds are fixed per draw — so playing more draws does not improve them, it just multiplies your exposure to the same edge.

Things people get wrong

"Some numbers are due"

They are not. Each draw is independent and the machine has no memory. A number that has not appeared in fifty draws is exactly as likely to come up next draw as any other — every number sits at 25% every draw, always.

Our own archive demonstrates this better than any argument. Over five draws, the gap between the most and least drawn number is around 100 percentage points. Over 220 draws it collapses to about 15, converging on the 25% baseline. Move the window yourself and watch it happen.

"Picking unpopular numbers improves my odds"

It does not change your chance of matching. In games where a prize pool is shared between winners it can affect how much you would split, but the probability of winning is identical either way.

"There is a system"

There is not. Every combination of numbers is equally likely, and no selection method — patterns, wheeling, frequency analysis, birthdays — changes the arithmetic. See the odds.

"Hot pairs come up together"

Numbers are drawn independently within a draw. Our pairs page publishes the most frequently drawn pairs and explains, on the same page, why the leader means nothing: there are 3,160 possible pairs, so something has to come top.

"A bigger multiplier means a better chance"

No. It means a bigger prize if you win. The odds are unchanged.

Keno compared with Lotto

If you are used to Lotto NZ, three differences matter. Keno draws four times a day rather than twice a week, so the rhythm is completely different. You choose your own spot count, which Lotto does not let you do. And Keno pays on partial matches far more often, with correspondingly smaller prizes.

The similarity that matters most: both carry a fixed house edge, and neither can be beaten by how you choose numbers.

Playing sensibly

Keno has a fixed house edge, which means that over time the game returns less than it takes in. That is the design, not a flaw, and no way of playing changes it. Treat what you spend as the cost of the entertainment rather than an investment, decide your limit before you start, and stop there.

With four draws a day, set limits per day or per week rather than per draw. A limit that resets four times a day is not really a limit.
