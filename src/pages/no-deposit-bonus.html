A no deposit bonus is a small credit — a free chip, cash, or free spins on registration — given for opening an account, with no deposit required. In New Zealand they turn up as 20, 25, 50 or 100 free spins no deposit, or as a NZ$5 or NZ$10 no deposit bonus. Comparison sites treat it as the most generous offer in gambling. It is closer to the opposite: the most precisely engineered one, and the only one where working out the answer takes a single number.

No deposit bonus max cashout, explained

Every no deposit bonus has a cap on what it can become, regardless of what you win with it. It is called maximum conversion, maximum cashout or maximum withdrawal, and it is typically somewhere between NZ$50 and NZ$150 on a NZ$10 to NZ$20 credit.

That cap, not the bonus, is the offer. If you take NZ$20 of free spins with a NZ$100 conversion cap and 50× wagering, and you hit NZ$4,000 on the third spin, you withdraw NZ$100. The other NZ$3,900 is removed by a clause, not by bad luck.

the offer is worth, at most, the conversion cap — never the amount you win

So the first thing to read is not the bonus size and not the wagering multiple. It is the cap, because it is the ceiling on every possible outcome.

What it costs you, honestly

This is the one place where I will not repeat the standard warning, because it does not apply.

On a deposit match you stake real money, so the wagering requirement has a real expected cost — the arithmetic is on the bonus page, and most welcome offers come out negative. A no deposit bonus is different in kind: you have put in nothing, so there is no deposit to lose. In pure money terms the expected value cannot be below zero.

What it costs is not money.

  • Your identity. Verification is required before any withdrawal, so the bonus buys a verified account — name, date of birth, address, document images — held by an offshore company you have not yet decided to trust.
  • Your contact details, and with them a marketing relationship. Operators do not give these away to be generous; they give them away because the lifetime value of the accounts that convert exceeds the cost of the ones that do not.
  • Your time, which is the largest cost and the least counted. NZ$20 at 50× is NZ$1,000 of turnover, and at NZ$0.50 a spin that is two thousand spins to reach a capped NZ$100.
  • The habit. The offer exists to get you playing on a platform you would not otherwise have opened. That is the whole business case, and it usually works.

Which makes the honest framing this: a no deposit bonus is not free money, and it is not a trick. It is an acquisition cost, paid to you, in exchange for something you may or may not want to sell.

Worked example: 20 free spins no deposit NZ

Take a representative offer: NZ$20 in free spins, 50× wagering on winnings, NZ$100 maximum conversion, seven-day expiry, pokies only.

  • Turnover required: NZ$20 × 50 = NZ$1,000.
  • Spins at NZ$0.50: 2,000. In seven days.
  • Expected balance after NZ$1,000 of turnover at a 4% edge: roughly NZ$0 — the requirement is calibrated so the average player clears it with nothing left.
  • Best possible outcome: NZ$100, the cap.
  • Worst possible outcome: NZ$0, and you have not lost any of your own money.

That third line is the design. A 50× requirement on a 4% game consumes twice the bonus in expectation, which is exactly why operators can afford to hand these out at scale. The small proportion of players who do come out ahead are capped on the way out.

How to claim a no deposit bonus, and what to check first

  1. Maximum conversion. The ceiling on every outcome. Read it first.
  2. Wagering, and what it applies to. On a free-spins offer it usually applies to the winnings, not the nominal bonus — so a big first spin creates a big requirement.
  3. Game restrictions. Frequently one nominated title. Check its published RTP in the game’s information panel; a low-return game makes the requirement more expensive to clear.
  4. Expiry. Convert the turnover into spins at the permitted stake, then decide whether it is achievable in the window.
  5. Maximum bet while wagering. One oversized spin voids the bonus and everything won with it, and it is enforced automatically.
  6. Whether a deposit is required to withdraw. Some terms require a qualifying deposit before the bonus can be cashed out at all, which quietly makes it a deposit offer.

That last one is worth the read. An offer marketed as no deposit that cannot pay out until you deposit is not a no deposit bonus, and the clause is rarely on the landing page.

Can you actually keep what you win?

Up to the cap, yes. “No deposit bonus keep what you win” is a real phrase in the market and a slightly misleading one: you keep what you win up to the maximum cashout, after the wagering is cleared. An uncapped no deposit offer would be a standing invitation to arbitrage, so essentially nobody offers one.

Two variants are worth knowing apart. A no wagering no deposit offer pays winnings as withdrawable cash with no turnover attached — rare, usually tiny, and genuinely the best version of this product. A free spins no deposit, no card details offer skips payment registration at signup, which is a privacy improvement at the front end and changes nothing at the back: verification still happens before any withdrawal.

What about bonus codes? A no deposit bonus code NZ players are given is usually just a tracking string for a particular acquisition channel. An “exclusive” code is exclusive to the publisher who issued it, not better for you — the terms underneath are typically identical, and the cap certainly is.

The verdict I would give a friend

Take one if you were going to open an account at that operator anyway. It costs you nothing in money, it is a cheap way to see how the platform and the cashier behave before any of your own funds are involved, and completing verification early is the single best thing you can do for withdrawal speed later.

Do not chase them. Collecting no deposit bonuses across many sites means handing identity documents to a series of offshore companies for a capped return that averages close to nothing, and the aggregate outcome of that is a lot of accounts, a lot of marketing email, and roughly no money.
