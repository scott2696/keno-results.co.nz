Why the prize column is blank

Most Keno calculators ship with a paytable baked in, and return an RTP figure to one decimal place. That figure is precise and meaningless, because the paytable is invented — or borrowed from a different operator in a different country.

We do not publish prize values, for the reason set out on how prizes work: they depend on your stake and Lotto NZ’s current schedule, and a figure we cannot guarantee is worse than none. So the calculator asks you.

Take the prizes from your ticket or the operator’s schedule, type them in, and what comes back is a genuine expected value for that paytable rather than a plausible-looking guess.

What is fixed and what is not

The probabilities are fixed. They follow from the rules — 20 numbers drawn from 80 — and are identical for every player, every draw, every operator. Nothing changes them. They are computed here with the hypergeometric formula, the same one shown on the odds page.

The prizes are not fixed. The operator sets them and can change them.

So the odds half of this page is permanent, and the return half is only as good as the numbers you feed it.

Reading the house edge

If you enter a full paytable you will get an RTP below 100% — that gap is the house edge, and it is how the game is designed to work. It is disclosed, legal and unavoidable.

The figure worth sitting with is not the percentage but the last line: what it costs over a thousand tickets. Keno draws four times a day, so a thousand tickets is not a hypothetical for a regular player.

No paytable beats the edge

Changing spot count moves the shape of the ladder, not the edge. Neither does number selection, the multiplier, or anything else. If a calculator anywhere shows you a Keno bet with an RTP above 100%, its paytable is wrong.
