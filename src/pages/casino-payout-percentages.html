Payouts and RTP, explained properly

What a 96% return-to-player figure actually promises, what it does not, and why two games with the same RTP can feel nothing alike.

{subnav}

RTP is the most quoted number in online gambling and the most widely misunderstood. It is quoted as reassurance — 96% comes back — when what it actually describes is the rate at which money does not. This page is about reading it correctly, which is a more useful skill than finding a site that advertises a higher one.

RTP and house edge are the same number

Return to player is the proportion of everything staked that a game pays back across its full theoretical cycle. The house edge is what is left.

house edge = 100% − RTP

A 96% pokie has a 4% edge. A 97.3% roulette wheel has a 2.7% edge. They are one fact stated from two directions, and the industry tends to quote whichever one sounds better in context — RTP for pokies, house edge for table games. Convert everything to the same side before comparing anything.

What “across the full cycle” means

This is where the number gets misread. A 96% RTP is calculated over the game’s complete theoretical distribution — often tens of millions of spins — not over your session, your night, or your year.

Consider what that implies. A pokie paying a 5,000× top prize on roughly one spin in two million holds a substantial share of its entire return inside an event most players will never see. Strip that prize out and the RTP experienced by a typical player is materially lower than the advertised figure, even though the advertised figure is completely accurate. Nothing is being hidden; the average is simply being carried by an outcome almost nobody gets.

The practical reading

RTP tells you the long-run cost of staking money on a game. It tells you nothing about tonight, and it is not a schedule — a game is never “due” to return to its RTP, because each round is independent and the average is approached only by adding more rounds, never by correcting past ones.

Where the cost actually comes from: turnover, not deposits

The number that determines what a session costs is not what you deposited. It is everything you staked, including money you won and staked again.

Deposit NZ$100, play NZ$2 spins on a 96% pokie, and win and restake along the way, and your total turnover before the balance runs out averages around NZ$2,500 — roughly your deposit divided by the house edge. Every dollar of that turnover is exposed to the 4%. That is why session length, stake size and spin speed matter far more to the outcome than the difference between a 95.5% and a 96.5% game.

expected total turnover ≈ bankroll ÷ house edge

It is also why the arithmetic on the bonus page works the way it does. Wagering requirements are stated in turnover precisely because turnover is where the edge is collected.

Volatility: the number that decides how it feels

Two games can both return 96% and be completely different products.

Both cost 4% of turnover. The high-volatility game just delivers that cost in a lumpier shape, which means the median session is worse than the mean even though the mean is identical. If you have ever felt that a game “paid nothing all night” despite a good RTP, volatility is the reason, and it is a design decision rather than a malfunction.

Hit frequency is not RTP

Hit frequency is the share of spins that return anything at all, and marketing material uses it interchangeably with generosity. It is not the same thing. A game that returns your stake on 30% of spins and slightly less than your stake on most of those is hitting constantly and returning very little — the “losses disguised as wins” pattern, where a NZ$2 spin returns NZ$0.60 with a celebration animation. Judge the return, not the noise.

Does the operator choose the RTP?

Sometimes, and this is worth knowing. A number of studios ship the same title in several RTP configurations — a 96% build and a 94% build of an otherwise identical game — and the operator selects which to run. The game looks the same, the paytable looks the same, and the difference costs you 2% of everything you stake.

The defence is simple: read the RTP in the game’s own information panel, on the site you are actually playing on. The figure on a review page is a figure about a different deployment of the same game.

Audited payout percentages are not about you

Sites sometimes publish a monthly audited payout percentage, often around 96–97%. It is generally real and independently checked, and it is also close to useless for your decision. It is an aggregate across every player and every game on the platform, weighted by turnover. A site whose volume is dominated by low-edge table play will show a higher figure than one dominated by pokies, without a single game differing between them.

The per-game figure in the information panel is the one that applies to what you actually do.

House edge across the common games

These are derived from the rules, not quoted. Single-zero roulette pays 35:1 on a straight-up bet against 37 pockets, so the edge is 1 ÷ 37 = 2.70%; the double-zero wheel pays the same 35:1 against 38 pockets, so 2 ÷ 38 = 5.26%. Adding one pocket doubles the cost.

Blackjack’s figure carries a condition the others do not: it assumes correct basic strategy and favourable rules. A table paying 6:5 on a natural blackjack instead of 3:2 adds roughly 1.4% to the edge on its own, turning the cheapest game on the table into a mid-range one. Rules matter more than game choice.

Where Keno sits

The same arithmetic runs this whole site. Keno’s probabilities are computed from the rules of the draw and published exactly, and the return calculator will give you the real expected return for a paytable — but only if you supply the prize values, because Lotto NZ does not publish them in a form worth quoting. A calculator that ships with an invented paytable gives you a number to four decimal places about a game nobody offers.

That refusal is the same standard applied here. Where a figure can be derived, it is derived and shown. Where it cannot, it is not asserted.

What none of this changes

Every RTP on this page is below 100%. Understanding the number lets you choose a cheaper form of the same entertainment; it does not produce a winning strategy, because there is not one to find. Decide your limit before you start.
